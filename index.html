The $40 rod is the fuse on a $1,400 tank

A water heater gets judged by gallons and first hour ratings, and the part that decides whether it reaches year 6 or year 20 screws into the top and costs about $40. The anode rod is a steel core wrapped in magnesium or aluminum, and it exists to fail. Electrolysis inside a tank of water eats the most reactive metal it can reach. The rod is the most reactive metal in the tank, so the corrosion budget gets spent on it and the steel liner keeps its skin. That is why the parts diagram calls it sacrificial.

The arithmetic is one plumbing visit, repeated. A builder grade 50 gallon tank lands around $1,400 installed with a 6 year tank warranty. A rod depletes in about 5 years of city water. A plumber who swaps the rod and flushes the sediment bills around $180 for the visit, $40 of part and $140 of labor. Three visits, at years 5, 10 and 15, keep a live anode in the tank for two decades: $1,400 + 3 x $180 = $1,940 total, which is $97 a year for hot water hardware.

The alternative is letting the tank be the consumable. Without rod care the liner itself is the part that rusts, and two tanks across 20 years, $1,400 each, runs $2,800, or $140 a year. The rod schedule saves $43 a year, every year, and the whole program costs $540 in visits. Priced per decade the maintained tank runs $970 against $1,400 for the neglect plan, a 31 percent premium for never opening the port on top.

A spent rod sends warnings in order. Hot water develops a sulfur smell. Taps run rust colored. Then the jacket drips. At the smell the fix is still the $180 visit. At the drip the liner is gone and the number becomes $1,400, a day without hot water, and a hauler for 130 pounds of dead steel. Sediment speeds all of it up, which is why the flush rides along on the same visit, 15 minutes on a garden hose.

Most tanks never see year 15 because the rod has no reminder sticker and the warranty expires before the first check is due. The 6 year warranty is the anode life expectancy restated in legal language. The maker prices the tank expecting the rod to go unwatched. A house that spends $180 every five years buys the cheapest hot water available per year, and the part doing the work is the least advertised object in the mechanical room.
